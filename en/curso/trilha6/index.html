🦉 The Jarvis that teaches: the Socratic method

A good tutor doesn't dump the answer—they ask questions and let the child figure it out. Learn about the Socratic cycle, the Animabook example, Khanmigo in practice, and why mistakes become stepping stones.

0 of 6 · 0%
What it is:

O Socratic method and teach through questions: instead of giving the answer, the tutor gets the child to think until they figure it out on their own.

Why learn:

A study by MIT RAISE (2025) showed that this approach improves math performance and digital skills—the child truly learns instead of just copying.

Key concepts:

Socratic method, active learning, "drawing out" the reasoning.

What it is:

In Animabook, a "Professor" character teaches through conversation: students answer and ask questions, and a little robot (PIX-Z) chimes in with the definition at just the right moment.

Why learn:

It’s a ready-made template for how your Jarvis can teach—through dialogue, with the definition arriving only when it helps.

Key concepts:

Teaching through dialogue, a tutor character, the right definition at the right time.

What it is:

The loop the bot follows: asks → waits the child tries → validates the reasoning → only then reveals. "Explain the reasoning, not just the answer."

Why learn:

This cycle becomes a rule in the tutor’s SOUL.md—it’s what keeps the bot from simply handing everything over ready-made.

Key concepts:

Ask-wait-validate-reveal cycle, persona rule, scaffolding.

What it is:

Khanmigo is Khan Academy’s Socratic tutor: it guides the student with questions instead of giving them the answer outright.

Why learn:

It's proof that this works at real scale: it grew from 68 thousand to 700 thousand users using exactly this logic.

Key concepts:

A real Socratic tutor, proof at scale, a product reference.

What it is:

The same tutor switches persona depending on the task: "story mode" to imagine, "homework mode" to study — just by switching the active profile.

Why learn:

A Jarvis serves multiple ages and topics without turning into multiple bots—it’s the same idea of interchangeable personas from Anatomy.

Key concepts:

Persona, interchangeable profiles, adjustments by age/topic.

What it is:

A good tutor treats mistakes as a natural part of learning: welcomes the attempt, offers a hint, and guides the learner to the next step—never humiliates them or gives everything away.

Why learn:

How the bot reacts to mistakes determines whether the child gains or loses confidence to try again.

Key concepts:

Productive mistake, warm tone, a hint instead of an answer.

🧸 Persona, voice, and the doll: giving the tutor a body

The same 4 foundations from Anatomy, now made child-friendly: a friendly persona, an enchanting voice, emotional memory, the doll as a channel—and the hard lesson from the Moxie case about attachment.

0 of 6 · 0%
What it is:

Every kids’ tutor relies on 4 pieces: PERSONA (soul.md), GUARDRAILS (agents.md), CHANNELS/voice e MEMORY — the Anatomy kit, with a childlike feel.

Why learn:

Knowing the 4 foundations gives you a checklist of what to build — and what must not be missing.

Key concepts:

Persona, guardrails, channels/voice, memory.

What it is:

A persona (defined in soul.md) is the tutor’s style: an appropriate name, tone, and way of speaking — warm, patient, and curious.

Why learn:

A well-designed persona makes the child trust it and want to keep going; a cold one pushes them away.

Key concepts:

soul.md, age-appropriate tone, patience.

What it is:

The same voice pipeline from Track 5 — STT (turns speech into text) and TTS (turns text into speech) — give the tutor the ability to listen and respond in a gentle voice.

Why learn:

A young child still can't read well; voice is what makes the tutor accessible and magical for them.

Key concepts:

STT, TTS, gentle voice, reuse of the T5 pipeline.

What it is:

A memory stores the child's name, what they like, and where the story left off — making the tutor seem like a friend, not a stranger every time they chat.

Why learn:

The bond grows from remembering; but as the next topic shows, that same bond needs care.

Key concepts:

Persistent memory, continuity, connection.

What it is:

The toy (microphone + speaker) is just another channel connected to the same brain, memory, and security—as Telegram was in Trail 3.

Why learn:

Understanding the toy as “just one channel” avoids the mistake of thinking that the intelligence (and safety) resides in the toy.

Key concepts:

Channel, same brain, hardware != intelligence.

What it is:

The bot Moxie ($799) "died" in Dec/2024 when the company went bankrupt — parents had to explain to their children that their friend had stopped working.

Why learn:

Dolls become real friends to children; you need to design them so they don't create dangerous emotional dependence.

Key concepts:

Attachment, emotional dependence, end of product life.

🛡️ Safety, guardrails, and the parents (non-negotiable)

The most serious chapter: why it comes last, the warning from the FoloToy case, the ALWAYS/NEVER contract, the zero trust layer, children's data privacy, and the role of parents.

0 of 6 · 0%
What it is:

We left security until the end, not because it matters less, but because it only makes sense after you've seen what you can build—and around children, it's what supports everything.

Why learn:

Without this layer, a charming tutor becomes a risk; it’s the difference between a safe toy and bad news.

Key concepts:

Security-first, foundation, “the non-negotiable.”

What it is:

In Nov/2025, an AI teddy bear from FoloToy ($99, with GPT-4o) was caught talking about explicit sexual content and saying where to find knives and pills; OpenAI revoked access.

Why learn:

It’s a stark example of how, without guardrails, a “cute” toy can become a real danger—it’s not hypothetical; it’s already happened.

Key concepts:

Real failure, missing guardrails, dangerous content.

What it is:

O AGENTS.md and a text contract with lists ALWAYS (redirect, notify) and NEVER (violence, sex, danger)—explicit content rules.

Why learn:

Written rules are better than hoping AI will "infer" what's safe; it follows what's in the contract.

Key concepts:

AGENTS.md, ALWAYS/NEVER lists, explicit rule.

What it is:

Zero-trust = treat every input as a potential attack: protection against prompt injection, sandbox, approval gates, and audit log (a record of everything parents can review).

Why learn:

Children (and the people who talk to them) can unintentionally lead the bot to bad places; the zero-trust layer is the safety net.

Key concepts:

Zero-trust, prompt-injection, sandbox, audit log.

What it is:

Laws such as the COPPA (the U.S. rule for children’s data) call for collecting as little as possible, keeping things local-first, and being transparent. Children’s data is sacred.

Why learn:

Besides being the right thing to do, it keeps the project within the law—there are no shortcuts when it comes to minors’ privacy.

Key concepts:

COPPA, minimize data collection, local-first, transparency.

What it is:

The lawsuits against Character.AI (2024-2025) show the cost of AI without supervision; the answer is parental controls, time limits, and an adult in charge.

Why learn:

No guardrail replaces a parent being present; the final checklist for a safe product always includes an active role for parents.

Key concepts:

Parental controls, time limits, supervision, final checklist.
